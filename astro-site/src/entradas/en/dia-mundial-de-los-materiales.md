Materials have been humanity's silent companions throughout history. From our ancestors' stone tools to the advanced materials used in space exploration, their impact on society is undeniable.

**World Materials Day** is celebrated by the member societies of the Federation of European Materials Societies (FEMS) on the first Wednesday of November, to highlight how much materials matter in everyday life.

Their importance to our scientific and cultural development is far greater than it might seem at first glance: our evolution as a species has been conditioned, to a greater or lesser degree, by the materials available to us at each moment in history.

> Every aspect of our lives — transport, housing, clothing, communication, leisure, food, health — owes a great deal to the materials at hand.

Over the past two hundred years some two hundred thousand materials have been developed, with properties special enough to change society outright. They made the Industrial Revolution possible, and with it the shift from a feudal order of semi-slavery to the new order of liberties promoted by the American and French revolutions.

The progress of every technology that improves our wellbeing — and that has taken life expectancy from 35 years to over eighty in a single century — is tied to the availability of new materials designed, synthesised and characterised by scientists and technologists in an unprecedented cross-disciplinary effort.

Understanding a material is the precursor to advancing a technology, and the boldest ideas often stay on paper until the right material appears: internal combustion engines thanks to new steels, aircraft thanks to aluminium and composites, modern medical imaging, or the semiconductors that make all of electronics possible.

## A brief history

**Stone Age.** Our ancestors made rudimentary tools from stone, bone and wood. The choice of material directly affected how efficient and durable they were.

**Metal Age.** The discovery of copper, bronze and iron enabled major advances in agriculture, construction and warfare.

**Steel.** Mass production during the Industrial Revolution transformed construction and engineering.

**Plastics.** In the early twentieth century they reshaped manufacturing and daily life. From nylon to polyethylene, these versatile materials are everywhere.

**Semiconductors.** Materials such as silicon are the foundation of modern electronics. Without them there would be no computers and no phones.

**Nanotechnology.** Nanomaterials, with unique properties at the nanometre scale, promise to reshape medicine, energy and manufacturing.

## Present and future applications

**Implants.** Biocompatible materials such as titanium are used in dental and orthopaedic implants.

**Smart biomaterials.** Materials that respond to stimuli like temperature or pH can release drugs in a controlled way.

**Solar cells.** Photovoltaic materials turn sunlight into electricity.

**Batteries.** Research into solid-state batteries and supercapacitors could transform energy storage.

**Self-healing.** Imagine a car whose bodywork repairs itself after a scratch.

**Shape memory.** Some alloys can return to their original shape after being deformed.

## To close

Materials are the backbone of technology and innovation. As we explore new territory, it's worth remembering that our evolution is bound up with the materials we choose. So next time you hold your phone or look up at the night sky, think about the materials that make that moment possible.
