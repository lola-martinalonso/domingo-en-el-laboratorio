ASTM standards define fatigue as the general term used to describe the behaviour of materials subjected to repeated cycles of stress or strain that cause a deterioration of the material through progressive fracture.

A material under repeated or fluctuating stress will fail at a stress far lower than the one required to produce fracture under a constant load. In other words: the repeated application of a stress causes progressive deterioration, while that same stress applied statically has no permanent effect at all.

The deterioration shows up as cracks that may eventually lead to rupture. Fatigue occurs under all kinds of loads and at both high and low stresses, but it only affects parts subjected to repeated demands of a certain amplitude.

> It is the leading cause of failure in metals — around 90% — although it also happens in polymers and ceramics.

## The three stages

Fatigue shows up as plastic deformation, called microplastic deformation in its smallest expression. The damage grows under continued stress until it causes final rupture. The process splits into three stages:

**Nucleation.** Occurs when the applied stress is lower than the elastic limit, the maximum stress a material can bear without permanent deformation. It starts where stress concentrations cause cyclic plastic strain: an accumulation of damage on microstructural features, surface or volume defects, which produces a crack at what is called the *initiation point*. This is usually where the grains have the greatest degree of freedom — notches, surface scratches, inclusions and pores.

**Propagation.** This covers most of the duration of the process and has two phases. In the first, the crack spreads very slowly and, in polycrystalline metals, along crystallographic planes of high shear stress. In the second, the rate rises dramatically and the crack stops growing along the axis of the applied load to grow perpendicular to it, through a blunting-and-sharpening process at the tip caused by the stress cycles.

**Rupture.** As the crack widens, the tip advances by continued shear deformation until it reaches a blunted configuration. A critical crack size is reached and rupture occurs. It looks brittle even in ductile metals, since there is hardly any associated plastic deformation.

## Fatigue testing

Components under cyclic loading can have a limited service life. Before certain critical components are put to use, a life assessment is carried out through fatigue tests that make it possible to predict how long the component will last.

In the test, a material or component is subjected to a cyclic load at a set frequency, chosen according to service conditions, in order to cause it to fail. Tests can be in tension or in compression. Fatigue damage can also result from cyclic thermal loads.

The process can become complex because of interactions between the applied loads and other damaging phenomena such as creep. Environmental attack, residual stress, corrosion, surface finish and geometric irregularities all have a significant impact on fatigue life.

## Where it happens

Fatigue failures appear in mechanical systems across the board: automotive, industrial machinery, electronic equipment. It affects components as simple as a bolt and as complex as a space shuttle.

- A point on a train wagon wheel is put under compression as it touches the rail; as it moves away from the bottom, the force drops. This happens once per rotation, millions of times in a single journey.
- Traffic crossing a bridge applies a fluctuating load: the bridge carries the greatest buckling load when traffic is nearest the middle, and that force disappears when there is no traffic.
- A ship's hull is under constant tensile and compressive forces as it rides over waves, especially pronounced in bad weather.
- Rotating machine components, springs, aircraft wings under repeated gust loads, or tyres deformed with every revolution.

The factors that govern vulnerability are stress concentrators (welds, drilled holes), the static or cyclic loads applied to the structure (wind, waves) and wearing phenomena such as corrosion or hydrogen embrittlement. When choosing a material for a particular application, the service conditions it will face are essential to consider.

## Three historic failures

**The Liberty Ship programme.** During the Second World War the Americans wanted to build ships at great speed and began using welded joints. Crossing the Atlantic, low temperatures made them brittle and many broke apart even before battle. This embrittlement happens because what allows a metal to deform without cracking is the movement of defects in its crystal lattice: in the cold, the energy needed to move them rises, so the defects stay put and accumulate stress until the material gives way abruptly. Being welded structures, cracks propagated between parts far more easily than if they had been riveted.

**The Aloha Airlines accident.** Fuselages face a different problem: external pressure at ground level and at ten thousand feet differ, and that change happens every time the aircraft takes off. Added to this are local changes at the wing joints from turbulence and flutter. The aircraft left the factory with a calculated service life of about 75,000 pressurisation cycles. It was found to have made 89,680 flights.

**Hip prosthesis failure.** Between 1973 and 1979, 805 patients received a full metal hip component, made of surgical-grade 316L stainless steel, at the Hospital for Special Surgery in New York. At least 21 needed treatment to remove the fractured femoral component: a failure rate four times higher than that of other designs. The origin is the fatigue loading of daily actions such as walking, jumping or running, where axial and bending loads combine into a cyclic compressive stress.

## Theories of failure

Although the problem has been known for a long time, it did not interest engineers until the mid-nineteenth century. In the twentieth century, Humfrey and Ewing observed that under dynamic loads slip deformations appeared similar to those in the static case, and that the progress of those lines led to rupture. Later, Hanson and Gough introduced the strain-hardening hypothesis to explain the existence of the fatigue limit: under small loads, hardening could compensate for and halt the advance of slip.

Today, although the hardening and slip theory is accepted, there is no quantitative formulation that allows a reliable calculation. Even so, the wealth of available data, especially for ferrous materials, has made it possible to develop highly reliable design methods. That is not the case for newer materials, which still require prototypes to be built and tested.

Every advance in understanding the process has greatly reduced failures and accidents. Even so, the phenomenon needs further study to reduce the need for high safety factors, which raise both manufacturing and maintenance costs.
