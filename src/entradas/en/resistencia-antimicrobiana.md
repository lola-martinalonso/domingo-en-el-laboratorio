Are we close to another pandemic? Bacterial resistance to antibiotics has been gaining ground quietly, causing thousands upon thousands of deaths every year around the world. What happens if we keep ignoring it?

Over the past few decades, driven by the misuse and overuse of antibiotics, antibiotic-resistant bacterial infections have emerged as one of the greatest challenges to public health **[1]**. Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is now widespread across 22 countries, with an estimated 500,000 people infected worldwide. The macroeconomist Peter O'Neill has warned governments everywhere that AMR deaths will overtake cancer by 2050, with an estimated 10 million deaths by then. On top of that, many bacteria sit inside cells in an active or dormant state, which makes it hard for antibiotics to reach them **[2]**.

One factor that makes infection even worse is the formation of *biofilms*. Biofilms are a major medical problem: this kind of ecosystem shields pathogenic bacteria from antibiotics and is one of the leading causes of chronic infection.

## A bill that keeps growing

Many experts are warning about where this is heading. Antibiotic resistance is growing at an alarming rate: some bacteria are already multi-resistant, meaning they do not respond to several different active agents. An EU survey found that 25,000 people died from infections with resistant pathogens in 2007. By 2015 there were almost 700,000 infections and 33,000 deaths, around 2,400 of them in Germany **[3]**. The US public health authority, the CDC, estimates 2.8 million antibiotic-resistant infections and 35,000 deaths in the United States every year, and according to the *Access to Medicine Foundation* the global death toll could reach 500,000, including 200,000 children.

Stephan Sieber, professor of organic chemistry at the Technical University of Munich, reports: *"Since 2000, only two classes of antibiotics with new mechanisms of action have reached the market."* Practically every conventional antibiotic attacks one of three targets in bacteria: it inhibits cell wall synthesis, or inhibits protein synthesis, or blocks two essential enzymes needed to replicate bacterial DNA **[3]**. For bacteria, a single mutation can be enough to develop resistance.

## Nanotechnology as an alternative

Following this logic, it is crucial to find a tool that acts on several levels at once. That is why nanotechnology has drawn attention for treating infectious disease. Unlike traditional antibiotics, which recognise specific cellular targets, the multi-target effects of silver nanoparticles make it much harder for the pathogen to develop resistance. Those mechanisms include: production of reactive oxygen species, denaturation of the DNA molecule, inhibition of the respiratory chain, and disruption of the cell membrane.

It is therefore vital to encourage research along these lines, so that a new pandemic cannot take over our lives again.
