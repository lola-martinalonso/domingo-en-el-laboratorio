An ideal ski gives the athlete a rigid, unyielding platform to fix their boots to, the flexibility needed to glide through turns, does not break under the pressure of jumps and landings, and is light enough not to slow the athlete down. And that is not all: skis have to resist impact damage, absorb the vibrations of freezing conditions, and withstand the extreme temperatures and intense sunlight so common in the mountains.

## A sandwich panel

To meet all those demands they are built as a **sandwich panel**, combining a core with reinforcements. Cores can be light titanium alloys, aluminium or several kinds of wood: maple, poplar, oak or aspen. These materials are layered to tune the ski to the desired level of strength, stiffness, flexibility and vibration damping, all at the lowest possible weight.

Wood, thanks to its excellent strength properties, is the choice for the highest-quality professional skis. Reinforcements are usually carbon or glass fibre, and **titanal** may also be included — an aluminium alloy whose isotropic behaviour keeps excellent strength in any direction, ideal for the turning and torsional forces a ski faces.

Carbon and glass fibres improve bending fatigue behaviour, and epoxy resins hold the core together so it works as one. Another material of great interest is **ABS** (acrylonitrile butadiene styrene), which absorbs vibration further and keeps its toughness down to -40 ºC. To prevent moisture absorption, a polyamide cover is used that also protects against ultraviolet radiation.

## The base

The base deserves attention too, since it is responsible for good glide. The most common material is **UHMW PE** (ultra-high molecular weight polyethylene), produced by sintering. Its low friction coefficient means it offers no resistance when gliding, it is very tough, it can be bent and flexed many times without breaking, it resists abrasion and it keeps its properties across a wide temperature range.

It also contains microscopic pores across its whole surface. Used as a ski's base layer, these pores act as a sponge for racing wax, tuning the ski to snow conditions.

To improve glide further, **graphite** is added to high-end skis and boards. Friction between base and snow generates static electricity, and polyethylene is not electrically conductive, so mineral fillers such as graphite stop that charge building up, reducing friction.
